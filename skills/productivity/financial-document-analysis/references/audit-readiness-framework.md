# Audit-readiness framework for broken management accounts

## When to use

When a user asks "what would make this account correct and ready for audit?" after you have
produced a corrected management account. This is the tiered checklist an auditor would use
to decide whether to sign off.

## Tier 1 — Blockers (auditor will not sign without these)

1. **Revenue verification** — every ringgit must be traced to a customer invoice, then matched
to a bank receipt. Aggregate revenue figures from ECOUNT are not sufficient.
2. **Cost of sales completeness** — COS must be plausible relative to revenue (compare to prior
year ratio). Intercompany payments to subsidiaries must be classified as: (a) subcontractor
COS, (b) management fee, or (c) intercompany transfer (eliminated in consolidation).
3. **Depreciation posting** — if a fixed asset register exists showing accumulated depreciation,
and the MA shows depreciation = 0, that is a material misstatement. Post monthly depreciation
from the fixed asset schedule before any audit.
4. **Tax computation** — RM0 tax on positive profit is not credible. Compute tax at the
statutory rate with capital allowances.
5. **Retained earnings update** — must be rolled forward from the last audited year through
the current period. Leaving it at a 2-year-old figure is a material misstatement.

## Tier 2 — High risk (auditor will qualify or emphasize if missing)

6. **Receivables aging** — trade receivables must have an aging analysis (0-30, 31-60,
61-90, 91-180, 180+ days). Provision must be adequate relative to the receivables balance.
7. **Intercompany reclassification** — payments to subsidiaries need invoices or management fee
agreements. Unexplained intercompany flows inflate apparent margins.
8. **Opening balances** — the ledger must start from the prior year's closing balance per
account, not from zero.
9. **Accruals and prepayments** — need schedules showing what each accrual is for, when
incurred, and when it will be paid.
10. **Inventory** — needs a stock count and valuation at period end. If no count was done,
the auditor will qualify the inventory figure.

## Tier 3 — Governance (auditor will emphasize or report to management)

11. **Preference share / RCCPS classification** — needs a proper accounting analysis under the
applicable framework (MPERS for Malaysian private entities). A CFO memo arguing for
reclassification to improve ratios is a governance red flag.
12. **Subsidiary audit impact** — if a subsidiary has a qualified audit opinion and negative
equity, the parent's consolidation must reflect this.
13. **Related-party disclosures** — ROU assets, deferred liabilities, and director loans need
full disclosure. "No fixed repayment terms" is a concern.
14. **Staff advances** — must be segregated from operating expenses. Need: who, how much,
when advanced, repayment terms, current balance. Some may be unrecoverable.

## Tier 4 — Housekeeping (auditor will note but not qualify)

15. Legends on every sheet explaining markers.
16. Opening balance row in the ledger.
17. Cross-references between flagged items and ledger rows.

## What would make it truly audit-ready

If the team completes Tier 1, an auditor could issue a qualified or emphasis-of-matter
opinion. Tier 2 gets them to an unqualified opinion. Tier 3 issues are governance
findings the auditor will report to management regardless.

The single biggest gap is usually revenue verification. Without invoice-level support for
the revenue figure, no auditor can sign off. That should be the first thing the team does.

## How to present this to the user

Group findings into three categories:
- **What makes sense** — verified bank cash, consistent P&L structure, cross-sheet formulas,
  flagging unverified items, catching zero depreciation/tax, catching intercompany gaps.
- **What does not make sense** — estimated revenue, implausible COS ratio, stale retained
  earnings, unaged receivables, unposted depreciation, missing opening balances, high
  percentage of unclassified transactions.
- **What's missing** — opening balances, bank reconciliation, receivables aging, fixed asset
  register with depreciation, inventory count, accruals/prepayments schedules, related-party
  disclosures, RCCPS analysis, subsidiary audit impact.

Be direct and honest. Do not soften findings for reassurance. State literally what an
auditor would accept and what they would reject.
